# Paws Are Typing… — comedy chat-skit channel bible

A new faceless YouTube channel, separate from "The Hidden Why". Each episode is a long-form (5–10 min, 16:9) skit told entirely through a group chat: cute animal-avatar friends, typing indicators, notification pings, reactions, and a story that escalates until one message wrecks everything.

## 1. Research summary

### Why chat skits in the Beluga style work
- **The whole story is text.** Beluga (Braden Boyko, 10M+ subscribers) built the format on Discord chats between an innocent-looking cat avatar and friends. The skits are usually about 3 minutes long, and the humour comes from fast, chaotic exchanges between characters [1][2]. With no voiceover, the joke is in the timing: a typing bubble that appears, stops, then appears again; a short silence; then the reply.
- **Message rhythm.** Real texting is short and choppy. A burst of 3–5 quick lines followed by one held line teaches the viewer to expect the punchline on the held line. Typing indicators and pauses work the way a beat does in stand-up.
- **Escalation, then "one message ruins everything".** A normal premise (a deadline, a plan) gets worse each act, and then one innocent line ("deleted the old files") turns it into chaos. The silence and the reactions after that line are the payoff.
- **Recurring characters.** Each avatar has a fixed texting style and a gag the audience waits for. Returning viewers get the pleasure of recognising it, and that builds loyalty to the channel rather than to one video.
- **Relatable premises and comment bait.** Titles in the genre read as "When you…" / "When a … " situations (for example the channel's "When You Can Only Use Emojis…") [3]. "Which friend are you?" premises get viewers tagging friends and arguing in the comments.
- **Brand safety is a real risk in this genre.** A family-media review of Beluga flags crude jokes and swear words that flash by in fast-scroll scenes [2]. Our rule is that every frame is clean, including fast-scroll gags, which keeps advertisers comfortable.

### Episode length and structure (our template)
| Segment | Time | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | 0:00–0:15 | Stakes card ("due in 14 hours") plus 2–3 messages that make the premise obvious |
| Act 1 | ~0:15–2:00 | Roll call / setup; each character's gag introduced once |
| Act 2 | ~2:00–4:00 | Scramble; a running gag repeats with a twist |
| Act 3 | ~4:00–5:30 | The "one message ruins everything" beat, then the fallout |
| Twist + tease | last ~1:00 | Reversal, callback to Act 1, then a "NEXT TIME" card |

Long-form at 6–8 minutes gives room for three acts and suits mid-roll ads later. Beluga's own uploads are shorter (about 3 minutes [2]), so length is one of the ways we differ.

### Titles and thumbnails in the genre
- Titles: "When…", "If…", "POV:", or a situation plus an ellipsis ("When You Can't Use the Letter 'E'…") [3]. Keep them under about 50 characters, as a plain situation, with no clickbait.
- Thumbnails: one or two avatars making big expressions, one large chat bubble or system line, and 1–3 words of text. Use high-contrast colours; the expression should read at phone size.

### Similar channels
Searches turn up many "Discord skit" uploads and how-to videos copying Beluga's format [4][5]. The genre is crowded with near-clones, so a distinct cast, world and premise style is what sets a channel apart.

### What YouTube rewards on a new channel
- **CTR, average view duration and viewer satisfaction** (likes, shares, survey answers, "not interested" clicks) are the main signals. Satisfaction now counts alongside watch time and has not replaced it [6][7].
- YouTube recommends videos whose view duration shows people found them interesting. Clickbait with low retention is less likely to be recommended [8].
- For a new channel, a CTR around 3–6% is commonly cited as healthy, though this is anecdotal [9]. Returning viewers and shares count for more than likes alone [6], and recurring characters plus "NEXT TIME" endings are aimed directly at returning viewers.

### "Inspired by a genre" vs copying, and the reused content policy
- A genre (group-chat skits with animal avatars) is not owned by anyone. Specific characters, names, catchphrases, server names, recurring bits and jokes are. **We copy none of these.** Our cast, server ("The Burrow"), gags and every line are original.
- YouTube's monetisation policy rules out **reused content**, meaning "taking someone else's content, making minimal changes, and calling it your own". The policy applies to the channel as a whole and is separate from copyright, so permission does not make reused content acceptable [10][11]. Reviewers look at how you "created, participated in, or produced" the content [10]. YouTube also expects creators to disclose altered or synthetic content where it applies [11].
- **How we stay safe:** every episode has an original script with a new premise, our own characters, and our own rendering and sound. We avoid template-swapped near-duplicates (the "repetitious content" risk), we never re-upload or compile clips, and we vary premises, structure and twists from episode to episode.
