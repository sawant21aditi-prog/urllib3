# Hook Playbook: Fear + Curiosity

Prepared 2026-10-05 by the virality agent for The Hidden Why (@thehiddenwhy).
Owner direction: **every Reel opens with FEAR + CURIOSITY.** Reel 2 example: beat 1 "The hole in your pen cap can save a life." and beat 2 "And no, it's not for the ink."

**Evidence** below means a cited study or a platform statement. **Estimate** means my judgement. The rules are written so the hook stays literally true and the page keeps the trust it needs for sends.

## 1. What the research says

| Finding | Source | What it means for us |
|---|---|---|
| Negative words lift clicks. In ~105,000 Upworthy headline variants (370M impressions), each extra negative word raised click-through by about 2.3%. **But words that read as *fear* lowered clicks** (−0.7% odds per SD), sad words raised them, and anger had no effect. | Robertson et al., *Nature Human Behaviour* 2023, via [Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/2023/03/negative-words-in-news-headlines-generate-more-clicks-but-sad-words-are-more-effective-than-angry-or-scary-ones/) · [paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-023-01538-4) | The fear has to come from **concrete stakes** ("save a life", "your toes", "fire"), not from scary vocabulary ("terrifying", "deadly secret"). |
| Curiosity gaps work only up to a point. A meta-analysis of 8,977 headline A/B tests found a curve: very vague teasers lose clicks, and so do headlines that give everything away. The best headlines give "just the right amount of information". | Aubin Le Quéré & Matias, *Scientific Reports* 2025, doi:10.1038/s41598-024-81575-9 ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/387761313_When_curiosity_gaps_backfire_effects_of_headline_concreteness_on_information_selection_decisions)) | Name the object and the stake. Hide only the **why/how**. "This one thing..." is too vague. |
| Meta reduces the reach of clickbait. It defines clickbait as posts that create misleading expectations by **withholding information or exaggerating**, often with phrases like "You won't believe...". | [Meta Transparency: Clickbait links](https://transparency.meta.com/features/approach-to-ranking/content-distribution-guidelines/clickbait-links/) | The payoff must be in the Reel and must match the hook. No "you won't believe". |
| Meta ranks "exaggerated or sensational health claims" lower. It finds them by spotting the phrases these posts typically use. | [About Meta, "Addressing Sensational Health Claims" (2019)](https://about.fb.com/news/2019/07/addressing-sensational-health-claims/) · [Meta: reducing problematic content](https://transparency.meta.com/enforcement/taking-action/lowering-distribution-of-problematic-content/) | Body or health stakes need a regulator, standard or peer-reviewed source, and a hedge. No cure, no diagnosis, no medical advice. |
| Instagram's recommendation guidelines exclude content that "trivialises themes around death", graphic imagery such as "deadly car crashes", and content rated false by fact-checkers. Repeat offenders stop being recommended as accounts. | [Instagram Help: Recommendations on Instagram](https://help.instagram.com/313829416281232) | Use paper-cutout **objects** to show the danger, never injury or crash imagery. One false-rated Reel can cost the whole account its non-follower reach. |
| Engagement bait such as share-bait ("share with 10 friends") and tag-bait ("tag a friend who...") is demoted. Requests made for a real reason, such as **raising awareness**, are treated differently. | [Meta: Engagement bait](https://transparency.meta.com/en-gb/features/approach-to-ranking/content-distribution-guidelines/engagement-bait) · [Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/facebook-demoting-engagement-bait/228071/) | Make the CTA useful: a safety reminder for a specific person or moment. Never "share if you agree". |

Every topic must have an obvious person to send it to. |

**Estimate:** fear + curiosity works best for us as a **protective reveal**: an everyday object has a hidden feature that protects you, or a warning you've been ignoring. The viewer feels a jolt of stakes, wants to know why, and leaves feeling smarter and safer. That emotional arc is what people DM. Pure dread isn't shareable.

## 2. Hook rules (apply to every Reel)

1. **Stakes come from the object, not from scary words.** Beat 1 names a concrete loss or rescue (a life, your toes, a fire, the air you breathe) tied to an object the viewer can touch. Banned words: *terrifying, deadly secret, horrifying, scary, shocking, you won't believe.*
2. **Say "you/your" and the object noun in the first four words, and show the object on frame 0.** The caption is on screen from frame 0, so muted viewers get the hook too.
3. **The hook must be literally true without the rest of the video.** Hedge the stakes with a modal ("can", "might", "often", "may") and keep the hedge in the caption. If a fact-checker clipped only beat 1, it should still pass. Beat 1 never states a death toll, a percentage or a cause that research.md rates below High.
4. **Hide the why, not the what.** Beat 1 names the object and the stake. Beat 2 is a reversal open loop that rules out the obvious answer ("And no, it's not for the ink.") or teases a second twist. The answer lands at 78–92% of runtime (the existing `payoff` device).
5. **Keep beat 1 short.** Aim for 8 words or fewer, with a hard cap of 10, and finish speaking by 1.5–1.6s (`claim_by`). Ten words at our TTS pace runs to about 2s, so cut to 8 whenever you can.
6. **Make the danger proportionate and resolve it.** Use only risks a regulator, standard, manufacturer or peer-reviewed study documents. Call rare risks rare. For health-adjacent topics (radiation, choking, fire), give the reassurance or the safety action by the payoff. Never leave the viewer scared, never give medical advice, and add emergency numbers wherever a life is at stake.
7. **No trauma visuals and no exploitation.** Show danger through objects and icons (a cap, a shoe, a bin, a smoke puff), never injured people, children in distress or crash wreckage. Don't hook on a tragedy from the last 30 days. Don't blame a brand: say "soft foam clogs", not a trademark.
8. **The CTA serves the fear.** The last beat turns the stake into a useful action: "Save this for <the moment you'll need it>" or "Send this to someone who <does the risky thing>". It loops back into beat 1. Never ask for likes, comments or tags.

### Clickbait line check (run before scripting)
- [ ] Is beat 1 true on its own, with the hedge spoken **and** on screen?
- [ ] Does the Reel actually pay off the exact promise in beat 1?
- [ ] Is the stake documented by a High-confidence source in research.md?
- [ ] Would a viewer who watched to the end feel informed, not tricked?
- [ ] Is there no injury imagery, no medical advice and no trademark blamed?

If any box fails, rewrite the hook. Don't rewrite the facts.

| ID | Template | Filled example (true) |

## 4. Reel 2 hook score

**Hook (new):** beat 1 "The hole in your pen cap can save a life." Beat 2 "And no, it's not for the ink."

**Score: 8/10.**
- **Strengths:** concrete stakes (a life) on an object within reach, "your", a hedge ("can"), and a reversal loop in beat 2. "Save" frames the fear as a rescue, which fits the Upworthy finding that fear words underperform. The claim is supported by BIC/ISO 11540 (F1–F5): the vent is designed so an inhaled cap doesn't completely block the airway.
- **−1, length:** beat 1 is 10 words, so it will probably finish around 2s, past `claim_by` 1.5s.
- **−1, precision:** "not for the ink" is slightly absolute. BIC says vented caps also "allow more air to circulate around the pen point" (F2). The old script hedged this as "isn't *mainly* for the ink".

**Alternatives (both true per research.md):**
1. Beat 1 "Your pen cap's hole can save a life." (8 words) / beat 2 "And no, it's not mainly for the ink."
2. Beat 1 "This hole can keep a choking kid breathing." (8 words; F3–F5: ISO 11540 covers pens likely to be used by children up to 14 and requires ≥8 L/min of airflow) / beat 2 "And the ink hole is somewhere else."

The new beat 1 no longer matches the old loop line (beat 13 "Send this to someone who chews every pen they touch."). It still loops semantically, because the chewer is the person at risk. If a word-level loop is wanted, end on "...because that hole in their pen cap can save a life."

### Reel 3: SAVE · "Why your smoke alarm chirps at 3 a.m."
Pillar: why_is_it_like_that · R5 S4 H4 V5 F5 = **23**
- **Verified facts**
  - Night chirps are usually a low battery or an alarm reaching end of life (typically 10 years). Homes cool by several degrees at night, and cold slows the battery's chemistry, which raises internal resistance and drops the voltage, so the alarm chirps. Source: [First Alert](https://www.firstalert.com/blogs/safety-corner/why-does-your-smoke-alarm-chirp-in-the-middle-of-the-night).
  - Kidde says homes are often coolest between 2 a.m. and 6 a.m. A weak battery can be enough on a warm afternoon but not overnight, and the chirp may stop when the house warms up, but the battery still needs replacing. Source: [Kidde](https://www.kidde.com/support/smoke-alarms/nighttime-alarm-chirps).
  - NFPA says a single chirp every 30 or 60 seconds means a low battery. If it keeps chirping after a battery change, the alarm is at end of life and must be replaced. Replace every alarm 10 years after its manufacture date. Roughly three in five home fire deaths happen in homes with no smoke alarms or none that work, and working alarms cut the risk of dying in a home fire in half. Source: [NFPA](https://www.nfpa.org/education-and-research/home-fire-safety/smoke-alarms).
- **Beat 1:** "That 3 a.m. chirp means your smoke alarm is dying." (9 words. The phrasing is true either way: the battery is low, or the unit is at end of life.)
- **Beat 2:** "And it's not bad luck that it's always at night."
- **Twist/payoff:** cold. The house is coolest around 2–6 a.m., and the weak battery's voltage dips just enough to trip the warning. Second twist: if it still chirps with a new battery, check the date on the back. After 10 years the whole alarm has to go.
- **CTA:** "Save this for the next time it chirps at 3 a.m."
- **Why people DM it:** everyone has stood on a chair at 3 a.m. hunting for the beep. Partners and roommates send it to each other, and adult kids send it to parents with "check the date on yours".
- **Cutouts:** smoke alarm (front and back with date label), 9V battery, alarm clock reading 3:00, thermometer dropping, moon, step stool.

### Reel 4: SEND · "What escalator brushes are really for"
Pillar: placebo_hidden_design / hidden design · R5 S5 H5 V5 F4 = **24**
- **Verified facts**
  - The brushes are skirt brushes, or skirt deflectors. They cover the small gap between the moving steps and the side panels, keep shoelaces and clothing from getting caught, and act as a cue to stay in the middle of the step. Their purpose is **not** cleaning shoes. Sources: [Elevator World](https://elevatorworld.com/blog/the-true-purpose-of-escalator-brushes/), [Reader's Digest](https://www.rd.com/article/escalator-brush/).
  - The US CPSC (May 2008) estimated about 11,000 escalator injuries in 2007, 10% of them from hands, feet or shoes getting trapped. It knew of 77 entrapment incidents since Jan 2006, about half with injury, and "all but two" involved "soft-sided flexible clogs and slides". Its advice: stand in the center of the step, tie your laces, avoid the sides. Source: [CPSC release 08-264](https://www.cpsc.gov/Newsroom/News-Releases/2008/Know-the-Steps-to-Safety-When-Using-EscalatorsSome-shoes-more-likely-than-others-to-pose-risk).
- **Beat 1:** "Those escalator brushes are guarding your toes." (7 words)
- **Beat 2:** "And one kind of shoe gets caught the most."
- **Twist/payoff:** soft, flexible clogs and slides. In CPSC's count, all but 2 of 77 incidents involved them. Rubbing your shoes on the brushes to "clean" them puts your foot right by the gap they guard. Stand in the middle.
- **CTA:** "Send this to someone who wears soft clogs everywhere."
- **Why people DM it:** it debunks something nearly everyone has done (shoe-polishing on the brushes), and the clog owner in every friend group is an obvious recipient. Parents of small kids forward it too.
- **Hedges:** say "US safety regulators once counted" (the data is from 2008). Never name a shoe brand in the VO or captions.
- **Cutouts:** escalator step and side panel, brush strip, soft foam clog, sneaker with untied lace, a magnified gap with a red marker circle.

### Reel 5: SAVE · "The hole in your plane window"
Pillar: why_is_it_like_that · R5 S4 H4 V5 F5 = **23**
- **Verified facts**
  - Airliner windows have several panes. A small "breather" or bleed hole near the bottom of the middle pane lets cabin air reach the gap, so during flight **only the outer pane carries the pressure** and the middle pane is kept in reserve as a fail-safe. Source: GKN Aerospace's Director of Technology, quoted in [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/why-is-there-a-hole-in-airplane-windows-1703660371). Also [Slate](https://slate.com/human-interest/2015/05/whats-that-thing-why-are-there-holes-in-airplane-windows.html).
  - The Boeing 737 maintenance manual says the air-gap design is also meant to "preclude fogging and frosting", and warns that with one pane cracked the "FAIL-SAFE FEATURE HAS BEEN ELIMINATED". Source: [Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/why-is-there-a-hole-in-airplane-windows-1703660371), quoting the manual.
- **Beat 1:** "Your plane window has a hole in it. On purpose." (10 words; cut to "Your plane window has a hole. On purpose." if timing is tight)
- **Beat 2:** "And the pane you're touching isn't the one holding you in."
- **Twist/payoff:** there are three panes. The outer one does all the work, the middle one (with the hole) is the spare that only works if it isn't already loaded, and the one you touch is just a scratch shield. The hole is what keeps the backup a backup. It also helps stop fogging.
- **CTA:** "Save this for your next window seat."
- **Hedges:** say "on most airliners". The failure of an outer pane is "extremely rare", so say so. Don't say "keeps you alive".
- **Why people DM it:** it turns a small flying fear into reassurance. People send it to the nervous flyer and to whoever they're travelling with next.
- **Cutouts:** airliner window frame, three-pane exploded view, magnified hole, frost crystals, toy airliner, sky clouds.

### Reel 6: SEND · "The dots on your microwave door"
Pillar: why_is_it_like_that / hidden design · R5 S4 H5 V5 F4 = **23**
- **Verified facts**
  - Microwaves are reflected by metal and pass through glass and plastic. They are non-ionizing and don't make food radioactive. The federal standard 21 CFR 1030.10 limits leakage to 5 mW/cm² at about 2 inches over an oven's lifetime. The FDA says not to use an oven whose door is bent, warped or damaged, or that keeps running with the door open. Source: [FDA, Microwave Ovens](https://www.fda.gov/radiation-emitting-products/resources-you-radiation-emitting-products/microwave-ovens). Also [eCFR 21 CFR 1030.10](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-J/part-1030/section-1030.10).
  - "The door/viewing window contains a metal grid with small holes through which optical radiation can pass but which contains the microwave frequency." Source: [Health Physics Society](https://hps.org/hpspublications/articles/microwaveoven/).
  - Ovens run at about 2.45 GHz, a wavelength of about 12 cm (c ÷ f = 12.2 cm, our arithmetic). The mesh holes are millimetres wide, far smaller than the wave, so light gets out and microwaves don't. Medium confidence: confirm the hole size on a real door with a ruler before stating a number.
- **Beat 1:** "Your microwave door is holding back radiation." (7 words. True: it contains microwave radiation. "Radiation" is a trigger word, so the caption should read "MICROWAVE RADIATION".)
- **Beat 2:** "And the tiny holes are the trick."
- **Twist/payoff:** the microwaves are about 12 cm long and the holes are a few millimetres, so the waves can't fit through. Light can, which is why you can watch your food. Then reassure: an oven in good condition is built to keep them in. A bent or damaged door is the one to replace.
- **Required early defuse (rule 6):** by beat 3 or 4 say "non-ionizing, not the X-ray kind" so the fear never turns into a health scare.
- **CTA:** "Send this to someone who watches their food through the door."
- **Why people DM it:** "Is it safe to stand near the microwave?" is a common worry, and this gives a satisfying physics answer. It also teases the person who stares into it.
- **Cutouts:** microwave front, door mesh macro, ruler next to a sine wave, light rays, a bowl of soup, a dented door (the warning case).

### Reel 7 (my call): SEND · "Why plane toilets still have ashtrays"
Pillar: disasters_redesign · R4 S5 H4 V4 F5 = **22**
- **Verified facts**
  - US certification rule 14 CFR 25.853(g): "Regardless of whether smoking is allowed in any other part of the airplane, lavatories must have self-contained, removable ashtrays located conspicuously on or near the entry side of each lavatory door." Source: [eCFR 14 CFR 25.853](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-25/subpart-D/subject-group-ECFR1e1f52030ba4797/section-25.853).
  - Varig Flight 820 (11 Jul 1973, Boeing 707, near Paris-Orly): a rear-lavatory fire filled the cabin with smoke, and 123 of 134 died. "A possible cause" was a lit cigarette thrown into the lavatory waste bin. The FAA then issued AD 74-08-09, which required no-smoking placards, announcements, "installation of ashtrays at certain locations" and inspections of waste-bin doors. Sources: [Wikipedia, Varig 820](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varig_Flight_820) (cites the AD) and [Simple Flying](https://simpleflying.com/why-airplnes-ashtrays-lavatories/) ("a mandated, last-line safety tool — ensuring safe disposal of illicit lit cigarettes").
- **Beat 1:** "One hidden cigarette can set a plane on fire." (9 words)
- **Beat 2:** "So no-smoking planes are required to carry ashtrays."
- **Twist/payoff:** the rule assumes someone *will* break the rule. An ashtray gives a rule-breaker's cigarette a safe place to go, instead of the paper-towel bin, the place investigators suspect a fire started in 1973. The safety feature is designed for the worst passenger.
- **CTA:** "Send this to the friend who's always on a plane."
- **Hedges:** use "possible cause" or "investigators suspected" for Varig. No crash or wreckage imagery. Don't mention vaping (not sourced here).
- **Why people DM it:** the paradox ("banned, but required") starts arguments in group chats, and frequent flyers will check the next time they fly.
- **Cutouts:** lavatory door with no-smoking sign, flip-lid ashtray, paper-towel bin, a cigarette with a smoke curl, a 707 silhouette (no wreckage), a rule book stamped 25.853.
