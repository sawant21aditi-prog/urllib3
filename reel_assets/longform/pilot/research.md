# Pilot research: "Why Elevators Don't Fall (and the Button That Often Does Nothing)"

The Hidden Why · long-form pilot · fact sheet · prepared 2026-10-07/08 by the Virality Agent.

Confidence key, the same as the Reel research files:
- **High** means a primary source, or several independent reliable sources, agree, and I read the page.
- **Medium** means one good secondary source, or a claim I saw only as a search-result excerpt, or sources that differ in detail.
- **Low** means keep it off screen.

**Rule for script.md:** every factual sentence has to cite an F-number from this file. If a claim is not in this file, it does not go in the script.

### A. Before the brake (1850s)

| # | Claim | Source(s) | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| F1 | Elisha Graves Otis was born on 3 Aug 1811 in Halifax, Vermont, and died of diphtheria on 8 Apr 1861, aged 49. He had two sons, Charles and Norton. | [Wikipedia: Elisha Otis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elisha_Otis) | High (standard biography; the dates match the Otis company histories) |
| F2 | By 1852 he was working at the Maize & Burns bedstead factory in Yonkers, NY, installing machinery. The factory needed a hoist to lift heavy equipment to an upper floor, and that "posed serious safety issues". Otis's answer was a hoist that halts automatically if the hoisting rope breaks. | [Wikipedia: Elisha Otis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elisha_Otis) | High for the outline. Medium for the exact factory name |
| F3 | How the brake worked, in an 1854 trade-journal description: the guide uprights carried "a row of rachet teeth". Pads were held back "by the tension of the rope". "In case of breakage, however, of the rope, the pads would immediately be thrown forward by a stiff spring, and, taking hold of the rachet teeth, would sustain the platform without injury." | Contemporary journal quoted by Lee Gray, [Elevator World, "Elisha Otis' 'Improved Elevator'" (Jan 2022)](https://elevatorworld.com/article/elisha-otis-improved-elevator/) | High (a contemporary primary source, quoted by the leading elevator historian) |
| F4 | Key design idea (our paraphrase of F3): the rope's own tension keeps the brake **off**. Lose the tension and the spring snaps the brake **on**. The safe state is the default. | Derived from F3 | High (a restatement, not a new fact) |

## 2. Misconceptions to avoid

1. **"All safe, gentlemen, all safe!"** This is unverified (F8). Do **not** voice it as a quote. If it is used at all, say: "Legend says he shouted... but no one wrote down what he actually said."
2. **"Otis invented the elevator."** He did not. Hoists and lifts existed long before him. He made the **safety brake** that made riding them trustworthy (F2–F5). Never say "invented the elevator".
3. **The demo was a single dramatic moment, with a velvet pillow and scissors.** That is embellishment (it appears in the LARB review but not in Gray's primary-source work). The *Tribune* says he cut the rope "occasionally", which means repeatedly (F6). Say "again and again".
4. **The location was the "Crystal Palace on 42nd Street".** We did not verify the street location, and it is not needed. Just say "New York's Crystal Palace".
5. **"The close-door button is fake / a placebo in every elevator."** Wrong. It is restricted by minimum door times (F20–F21), and it often works after them (F22, F25, F26). Some may be disabled by owners, but how many is **unknown** (F24). Always say "often" or "in many elevators".
6. **"The ADA banned close-door buttons."** No. The ADA standards set minimum open times. The same standards specify the button's tactile "two facing arrows" symbol and braille label (F37), so it is regulated, not banned. Say "the law made the doors wait".
7. **"Pressing the call button more makes it come faster."** No (F25). That is a useful side myth, but don't conflate it with door-close.
8. **"If the cable snaps you'll die" / "jump at the last second".** There is not just one cable (F31), and the jumping myth isn't sourced here, so don't include it.
9. **Betty Lou Oliver "proved the brakes don't work".** No. Her car's cables, including the safety cable, were severed by a bomber strike (F33), and why she survived is unsettled (F34). Frame her case as how extreme it took to defeat the system, not as evidence of danger.
10. **"80% of close buttons don't work."** This is unverified (F24). Use it only as a disputed claim, never as a stat card.

## 3. Claims to keep off screen

- The quote "All safe, gentlemen, all safe" as a fact (F8). The "velvet pillow / scissors" details.
- Any single explanation for Oliver's survival (F34). Any image of the crash, fire, wreckage or injured people. Use fog, a calendar and an indicator dial instead.
- The 80% figure as a statistic (F24).
- The dollar costs of the 1945 strike ($100M in lost taxes vs $8M a day): the sources differ. Use "1.5 million office workers" instead (F16).
- Exact Phase II wiring details beyond F28–F29. Don't teach anyone how to put an elevator into fire service, and never show a real key-switch model or keyway.
- Any claim that a specific brand's elevators have disabled buttons.
- The mirror story as fact (F36). It can be a hedged aside only.
- Present-day "elevators are X times safer than stairs/cars" comparisons: none are sourced here.
- "Otis invented the elevator", or "first elevator ever".

## 4. Imagery rules for this topic (real people)

- **Elisha Otis:** use only public-domain 19th-century portraits (e.g. Wikimedia Commons "Otis.jpg") or the period engraving of the 1854 demonstration ("Elisha_OTIS_1854.jpg" on Commons). Check the licence tag on each file before use.
- **Betty Lou Oliver:** don't use her photo. Show her only as a name card plus objects: a lift operator's lever, an indicator dial, a Guinness-style certificate drawn in our own style.
- **1945 strike, ads and operators:** use public-domain or Library of Congress images where the rights are clear. Otherwise use our own cutouts: a uniformed operator silhouette, a lever controller, a picket sign.
- No logos as accusations. The Otis wordmark may appear only in neutral historical context (the company name on a plaque), never as a gag.
